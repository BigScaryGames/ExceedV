**Scope:** AI-simulated playtest of Exceed v0.6 — Session 0 (character creation, 50+50 XP), Session 1 (one-shot "The Toll at Greywater Mill"), Session 2 (debrief). Evidence drawn from [[Session 0 - Party and Character Creation]], [[Session 1 - The Toll at Greywater Mill]], [[Playtest Statistics]], [[Session 2 - Debrief and Feedback]].

## Verdict in one paragraph

The tactical core is real and it works: the AP economy with domain-locked points, player-rolled defenses, Lines and Zones, and the NPC builder produced a session that *moved*. Two structural risks surfaced repeatedly: **the magic math** (cast bonus vs static DCs crits constantly, Strain never engages, and a 20-spell menu at start is unplayable in practice) and **the attribute-threshold economy** (near-misses everywhere, attribute-gated perks unreachable at the official "trained adventurer" budget). Below the headline items sits a layer of unwritten rules — fifteen rulings across two sessions — that any outside table would resolve differently.

---

## Strengths (with evidence)

1. **The AP economy produces planned turns.** Every player reported budgeting, not just acting; nobody exhausted 7 AP in any round, and turns were capped by targets and positioning rather than points. Domain-locked AP (Prowess/Magery surcharge slots) was used exactly as intended — strikes and casts.
2. **Player-rolled defenses keep everyone at the table on enemy turns.** Bram's two critical defenses (doubles on Deflect) were his favorite moments of the session — on *defensive* rolls. NPC-side combat was fast (static defenses, mooks don't roll).
3. **The NPC and Encounter Builder held up under fire.** Twenty minutes of prep; threat math matched difficulty labels (party weight 6 vs ~7 total threat = a professional, winnable job). Morale notes on statblocks produced the session's most alive moments (bowman flees at 2 HP, toll-boy runs when Pike limps).
4. **Lines and Zones ran clean.** Four zones sketched on paper, two line costs written down, zero positioning arguments. The players argued about fiction, never about measurement. For the 0.6 milestone's central question, this session votes yes.
5. **Ranged combat is the best-flowing subsystem at the table.** Distance DC + Aim + Fast Archer + range increments gave the archer player his most satisfying bow experience (his words). Skirmisher's Strike delivered the hit-and-run fantasy in one line.
6. **The two-pool XP split changes behavior.** The Life XP pool made the tavern gossip scene feel *paid* rather than a pre-fight snack; players self-sorted into social/exploration participation because it had a currency.
7. **Social mechanics can finish a fight.** Aid action + opposed negotiation + Intimidation ended the boss encounter with zero damage; the villain's morale statblock made surrender a *designed* outcome, not a GM fudge.
8. **Cheap spell breadth supports character fantasy.** Yara's 23-spell toolkit made her feel like a practiced village witch — breadth as flavor is cheap (1–3 XP). (See weaknesses for the cost of this same fact.)

## Weaknesses (with evidence)

1. **Magic numbers vs static defenses.** Yara's casting attack is 2d10+5 against static Dodge DCs of 12; she hit 4/4, crit 2/4 via beat-by-10 (rolls of 23–24 are routine, not lucky). Three critical casts in one session; the magic chapter's own warning ("spells having on average higher rolls vs defense rolls") is confirmed with interest. Extended crit range was designed for *contested* rolls; against fixed NPC DCs it fires constantly.
2. **Strain never happens at low tier.** Six casting checks, lowest margin +2, zero Strain. The entire magic risk economy (fail → strain → wounds) never entered play. DC 8 for tier 0 / 10 for tier 1 is beneath a trained mage's floor.
3. **The 20+-spell menu.** 1–3 XP per spell at character creation lets a starting mage buy breadth nobody can track. Yara's player — who *chose* those spells — could not enumerate them mid-session. There is no "prepared" or loadout concept to compress the menu.
4. **Attribute thresholds generate shelf purchases.** Four near-misses (pools of 28–29) across three characters; every player ended the build with attributes "one purchase from better, forever." Nobody was told this in advance, and it read as the machine keeping their change.
5. **Attribute-gated perks are locked at the official starting budget.** Backstab needs DX 2 (30 pool points) *and* Prowess 2 (30 domain XP) — impossible on 50 Battle XP. Shield Rush (Strength 1) only became legal at the end of Bram's build. Perks printed on the menu, locked in the kitchen.
6. **Mooks evaporate without mattering.** 1 HP + d4 sticks + shared slow slot: of five mooks fielded, exactly one ever rolled an attack (and hit, to be fair — the session's one scary moment). The tank wasted a full round on wolves that died to faster PCs. Mooks need a role beyond damage that stays at "full tier dice" on paper.
7. **Critical failures on attacks are undefined.** "Failure + doubles → critical failure (if applicable)." Twice in one fight (both genuine 2+2 rolls), the table had to invent consequences on the spot — and a miss that also punishes felt double-charged to the player.
8. **Critical defenses do nothing by default.** Two rolled (doubles), zero effect, per the rules' own admission ("succeeds beautifully" + silence). The most excited a defender got all night, mechanically unpaid.
9. **Healing overflow is discarded silently.** A crit heal of 12 returned 7 (caps) and deleted 5. Nobody objected in the moment, but it makes crit *healing* the worst possible crit outcome.
10. **Flaws need bait.** Kleptomania never triggered — the one unattended valuable was warded by plot. A flaw that never fires is negative XP with flavor text.

## Balance alarms (ranked)

1. **Extended crit range vs static NPC defenses** — fix the interaction (roll NPC defenses vs spells, cap extended crits at +1 die, or raise NPC Dodge vs spells). Evidence: 3 crit casts, 4/4 spell hits.
2. **Casting DC floor** — DC 8/10 vs a trained caster's 2d10+5 means no fail state, no Strain, no drama at tiers 0–1. Evidence: zero Strain in 6 casts, +2 worst margin.
3. **Surprise stun** — one leader roll stunned *every* ambushed NPC (16 vs 10/10/11 → all lose 3 AP). Powerful, possibly correct for the ambusher-fantasy, but it deleted an entire enemy wave's turn one; watch it in player-facing ambushes.
4. **Mook HP vs initiative** — see weakness 6; the wolves contributed one bite and one wasted player round.
5. **Table-wide Shaken from one NPC action** — flat DC 14 hit 2 of 3 PCs; swingy at Threat 2, consider magnitude caps.

## What this playtest did *not* exercise (honesty section)

The 0.6 design questions we couldn't answer: zone-control play never happened — no Shoving line, no Hold-the-line stances, no line effects placed, no Ready-stance use, no skirmish flips (both melees formed once and stayed). The Reaction economy was pure deterrence (zero defensive reactions triggered). A second playtest should be built *to* these questions: a chokepoint defense, a boss that repositions, enemies that force skirmish flips.

## Suggestions (evidence-linked, non-prescriptive)

1. Write rulings 1, 5, 8, 9, 14 into the rules files; they came up in the first hour of the first fight.
2. Compress the starting spell menu (prepared-cast loadout, or a "known vs ready" split) — 20+ known spells is a UX failure at 49 XP.
3. Re-price or re-gate attribute-valued perk requirements (Backstab) against the official 50 XP budget, or say explicitly "these are advancement perks."
4. Give critical defenses a default small effect and critical failures a defined consequence menu — both fired at this table on night one.
5. Give mooks a non-damage job (Shove, grab, scream-and-Shaken, body-block) and/or fold their slot earlier in initiative.
6. Tell players at character creation when a purchase lands 1–3 points short of a threshold — the spreadsheet shouldn't be required to see the cliff edge.
7. Consider letting crit healing overflow into Stamina (it currently evaporates).
